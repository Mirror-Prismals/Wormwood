A proposed material interpretation (not decided or implemented)

Use ground/stone support below the foundation; wood joists, planks and decking
for the stage and stepped seating platforms; framed or masonry wall panels;
and a roof structure whose beams/trusses actually span between supports. Metal
connectors and a suitable long-span roof system would be additional material
work. Ceramic, fabric/carpet and acoustic finishes can be separate, thin
attachments. The old ceramic-looking stage need not become solid ceramic.

The material choice stays open. Brick and mortar can be a branch of this
construction system, rather than a prerequisite for the first wooden shack.
The first useful milestone is a two-plank joint, followed by a supported floor
bay and a small shack. The full theatre should eventually be a plan assembled
through those same operations; its authored generator should not bypass them.

Necessary physical foundations

1. Stock and cuts. A felled trunk must retain its material coordinates and grain
   as it becomes beams/planks. Each piece needs mass, an occupied boundary,
   internal stiffness and fracture history. Existing workshop wood has a finite
   prepartition and rigid intact fibre bundles; it does not yet provide arbitrary
   sawing, beam flexure or an inventory of real cut stock.
2. Fasteners. A nail needs a head, shank, tip, metal response and a depth-dependent
   contact/embedding relationship with wood. Hammer work drives penetration;
   fibres yield and can split. Grip transfers shear and withdrawal forces, and
   can pull out or tear the wood. It must not switch to an indestructible weld
   on proximity. The OGC boundary gives contact forces; a separate material/joint
   law must determine embedding, friction, yielding and failure.
3. Structural response. Plank bending and joint compliance must transmit load
   through the structure to ground supports. Calm assemblies can use a coarse
   elastic representation; they still carry load and need failure checks. Local
   refinement belongs at impacts, joints and growing cracks. An unloaded camera
   region must not become an infinitely rigid or unbreakable support.
4. Masonry, if chosen. Individual bricks/stone retain brittle geometry. Fresh
   mortar is a distinct wet plastic material; curing changes its stiffness and
   adhesion over time. Bed joints carry compression, shear and limited tension.
   Contact, bonding and curing are separate phenomena. Demolition should use
   the same relationships as construction.
5. Manipulation. Supporting a plank, placing a nail and delivering hammer blows
   need accessible desktop/mobile intent controls, constrained hands, and real
   tool contact. The worker workshop is a place to make those actions legible
   before adding more player gestures.
6. Persistence and acoustics. Save stock identity, cut faces, plastic strain,
   fastener depth, joint damage and temperatures. Accepted structural geometry
   and its materials should supply the acoustic scene. A speaker rig's weight
   should enter its brackets and truss rather than an invisible world anchor.

Suggested next automatic construction demonstrations

A hammer drives one nail through a plank into another; load pulls the joint in
shear, then withdrawal; excessive impact splits along the grain. Repeat with
several nail depths. Then load a small plank-and-joist floor, remove a support,
and watch the load transfer and failure. These are proposed next steps, not
features delivered by the current workshop change. Metal, fasteners, mortar,
construction tools, structural assembly and theatre reconstruction remain work.
